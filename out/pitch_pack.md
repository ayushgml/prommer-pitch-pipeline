## Prompt Engineering Didn't Die, It Grew Up: The Case for Context Engineering
_Audience:_ Developer- and AI-engineering-focused podcasts and technical publications. Their listeners build LLM applications and agents and are working out how to get from a good demo to a reliable production system.

- Thomas argues that prompt engineering didn't die; it evolved into the broader practice of context engineering. [C7]
- In his view, clever prompts produce impressive demos, but well-structured context produces production-ready systems. [C8]
- He argues that reliable autonomous agents come from better context architecture, not better prompts. [C9]
- The foundation of context engineering, he says, is treating documentation as machine-readable context rather than only as reference material for humans. [C10]
- He sees the 2025 shift from vibes-based AI development to systematic context management as a lasting change, not a passing trend. [C11]
- For organizations, he frames the decision as how quickly to build context-engineering capability, not whether to invest in it. [C12]
- He brings a practitioner's view because he writes production code daily and ships AI-powered systems using Claude, GPT-4, and agentic coding pipelines. [C1]

## The Model Usually Works: Why AI Initiatives Really Fail and What to Do Instead
_Audience:_ Business, executive-leadership, and digital-transformation shows and outlets. Their audience of CEOs, CIOs, and senior decision-makers owns AI budgets and needs to know why so many initiatives stall.

- Thomas says that in every failed AI project he has audited, the root cause was never the algorithm but data quality, unclear objectives, or a lack of organizational readiness. [C16, C24]
- He argues that most organizations overestimate their data readiness and should budget 60–70% of project effort for data work. [C15]
- In his view, the model usually works, and most AI initiatives quietly die on adoption and trust. [C23, C18]
- He says the best AI projects start with a business problem, not with the goal of using AI, and should be pursued only if they can deliver at least a 10x improvement in a key metric. [C19, C22]
- He argues that general executive support isn't enough: AI needs a senior owner who will fight for budget when the first pilot disappoints, and transformation lands only when a single accountable owner drives it. [C20, C30]
- He says enterprises often stick to low-risk copilot tools to avoid model-risk governance. [C30]
- He favors a 90-day pilot with clear success metrics over a 12-month roadmap with vague outcomes, and deliberate maturity-building over fragile quick fixes. [C21, C17]
- His bottom line is that organizations winning with AI have the clearest strategy and the discipline to execute it, not necessarily the most sophisticated technology. [C13, C14]

_Audience:_ Engineering-leadership and CTO-focused podcasts and publications. Their audience of engineering managers, VPs, and CTOs is rethinking team structure, AI tooling, and how credit works when AI contributes to the work.

- Thomas argues that technology executives should stay hands-on builders, and he still writes production code daily in Python, TypeScript, and infrastructure-as-code. [C1]
